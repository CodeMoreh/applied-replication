Weights

Design weights, precision weights and population weights

The specification menu has a regression weight axis with four settings and a cell weight axis with two, and between them those two lines stand in for three different decisions. They sit at two different levels of the analysis, they are justified by different arguments, and only one of them changes what the analysis is estimating. Running them together under the one word is the sort of thing that makes a robustness check look like a technicality when it is really a change of question.

This module separates them. It is the natural companion to the causal graphs module and to what each decision commits you to, and it uses the course panel throughout.

The three decisions

The first is a measurement decision: it changes how well the number in the cell estimates the thing it names. The second is an estimation decision: it changes the efficiency of the fit and leaves the target alone. The third is an estimand decision: it changes whose average the coefficient describes, and two analysts who resolve it differently are answering different questions.

Design weights within the cell

Eurobarometer ships design weights with every wave, and they are not optional decoration. Fieldwork does not deliver a miniature of the population: some groups are easier to reach than others, some refuse more often, and the achieved sample tilts accordingly. The W1 national weight reverses that tilt so that the weighted sample matches the country’s known population margins for sex, age, region and size of locality.

Applying it changes the country-year means, though not dramatically here. Across the 270 cells, the largest change to the cosmopolitan scale is 0.0433 on a 0–1 scale whose mean is about 0.51, and the weighted and unweighted cell means correlate at 0.9939. That is small but not nothing: it is the difference between a country’s respondents and a country’s population.

ImportantEurobarometer ships more than one weight, and the choice among them is an estimand decision

A Eurobarometer file carries several weight variables, and they are not interchangeable. The national weight (W1) makes each country’s sample representative of that country. The European weights (W3, W4 and their relatives) additionally scale each country by its population, so that pooling all respondents together gives an estimate for the EU population as a whole. An extrapolation factor (WEX) grosses the sample up to population counts.

Reach for the wrong one and you will silently answer the wrong question. Using a European weight and then averaging countries double-counts population size; using a national weight and then pooling respondents across countries gives you an average over respondents, which is an artefact of how many interviews each country happened to get. Whenever a survey ships more than one weight, read the codebook before choosing, because the choice among them is the country-versus-person estimand decision arriving in disguise.

Effective sample size

Weighting buys representativeness and pays for it in precision. When respondents carry unequal weights, the weighted mean is less precise than an unweighted mean of the same number of interviews, because a few heavily weighted cases do a disproportionate share of the work. Kish’s effective sample size is the standard way of saying how much precision is left:

\[n_\text{eff} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}\]

For a cell where every weight is equal, \(n_\text{eff} = n\). As the weights spread out, it falls. In this panel the median cell has \(n = 1540\) interviews and \(n_\text{eff} = 1234\), a design effect of 1.09; the worst cell loses 41.3% of its sample to the weighting.

The practical consequence is that if you are going to weight cells by their precision, \(n_\text{eff}\) is the right variable and \(n\) is an approximation to it.

Sample size and population size

It is tempting to treat the respondent count (n_cy in the panel, n_cell on the menu) as a size variable, on the reasoning that bigger countries get bigger samples. They do not, or not by much. Eurobarometer fields roughly a thousand interviews in each member state whatever its size, with smaller allocations for the smallest states and a larger one for Germany. So across this panel:

  • the respondent count runs from 500 to 3614, a ratio of 7.2 to 1;
  • the adult population runs from 329,409 to 70,761,158, a ratio of 215 to 1;
  • and the two correlate at only 0.35 across the 270 cells.

Weighting by n_cell therefore does not make the analysis population-representative. It makes it precision-weighted, which is a different and much weaker claim. If you want the average European, you have to weight by population and say so.

The published specification under each weight

Here is C6HJR’s published Multi100 specification – cosmopolitan framing, two-way fixed effects, all years, raw unemployment, no co-predictor – fitted under all eight combinations of the two cell-level and four cell-weighting choices.

: C6HJR’s published Multi100 coefficient under every weighting choice. The first row is the published Multi100 result. {#tbl-c6hjr}

Three things are worth reading off that table.

Every row supports the claim and every row is significant, so nothing here is a sign flip or a disappearing result. What moves is the size: the partial correlation runs from −0.325 to −0.195, a spread of about two-thirds of the smaller value, on a specification that any methods section would describe in one identical sentence.

The two kinds of weight pull in opposite directions. Precision weighting by the respondent count weakens the result, from t = −3.80 to t = −3.04. Population weighting strengthens it, to t = −4.74. An analyst who reasons “I should weight, because weighting is more careful” without saying which argument they are making could land on either.

Kish’s correction changes almost nothing: n_eff and n_cell give t = −3.11 and −3.04. The design effects here are small enough that the distinction is a matter of correctness rather than of consequence. That is worth knowing in both directions: it is the right variable, and on this panel it will not rescue or ruin anything.

The denominator

There is a decision underneath all of this that the weights cannot reach. 26,325 respondents, 6.3% of the sample, mentioned nothing at all when asked what the EU means to them. The original paper and every reanalysis of it scored those respondents 0 on all four framing scales rather than dropping them, so a person with no opinion enters the data as a person with no cosmopolitan framing.

That would be a defensible convention if the no-opinion rate were constant. It is not:

: The four highest and four lowest no-opinion rates. {#tbl-noop}

An eight-fold difference in the rate of a response that is coded as a substantive zero is a measurement problem no weight will fix, because the weights correct the composition of the respondents, not the treatment of their answers. If you think the no-opinion respondents should be dropped rather than zeroed, that is an argument about what the outcome variable is, and it belongs on the measurement level of the specification menu rather than in the weighting row.

What to take away

  • Say which of the three decisions you are making, and give the argument that goes with it. “Weighted” on its own is not a specification.
  • Design weighting and precision weighting leave the estimand alone. Population weighting changes it, and a paper that switches to population weights in a robustness section has changed its question without saying so.
  • When a survey ships several weights, the choice among them usually encodes the country-versus-person decision. Read the codebook first.
  • Look at what your weight variable actually varies over before trusting it as a proxy for anything. Here the respondent count spans a factor of 7.2 and the population it is standing in for spans a factor of 215.

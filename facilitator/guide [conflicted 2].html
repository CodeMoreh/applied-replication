Times in the part tables are part-relative: 0:00 is the start of that part. The clock below maps them onto the next delivery.

You are two hours behind the room all day: the room’s 10:00 start is your 08:00. Everything you post in chat should carry the Romanian clock, because that is the one that the summer school published.

Three stretches of the deck are optional depth, marked on screen and skippable in one keystroke: the R tutorial (Part 2, 10 slides), the methodological tutorial (Part 3, 12 slides) and the multiverse tutorial (Part 4, 8 slides). Five slides in the tools segment of Part 1 carry the same mark. Decide on the day how much of each the room wants. The timings below assume you walk roughly half of each; walking all three in full costs about 25 minutes you do not have, and skipping all three entirely leaves the hands-on tasks with more room than they need.

5.1 Driving 148 slides

Key What it does
space · right arrow · clicker next slide. Always exactly one slide – the deck is in linear mode on purpose, so nothing that a clicker sends can skip a segment
d switch between the extended and the lean deck, live. In lean mode the optional slides leave entirely: every key skips them and the count drops from 148 to 113
] and [ jump to the next / previous part. Four parts, four jumps
g jump bar: type a few letters of any slide title, press Enter
s speaker view (notes, timer, next slide)
f · esc fullscreen · slide overview

The readout, top right. Two lines. The first names the part and gives both positions – PART 3 · 8/24 · 90/148. The second is a badge that is always present, so the state of the deck is never a guess:

Badge Means
green extended the full deck, and this slide is part of the main flow
burgundy optional the full deck, and this slide belongs to an optional run. The figure beside it is your position inside that run – 3/12
amber lean the optional runs are switched off
a burgundy dot before the amber badge lean mode, and an optional run has been lifted out right here – either the slide you are on is the last before it, or the first after it. Press d if the room wants it

The readout steps aside on the title slide, the part dividers and the break slides, where it would sit over the dark background beside the brand mark.

Opening the lean deck directly. Add ?depth=0 to the address and the optional runs are gone before the first slide appears – useful if you already know the room. Without it the deck opens complete. If you follow a link to a slide that is hidden in lean mode, you land on the slide that the run lets out onto, not on a blank.

The escape hatch on screen. The first slide of each optional run carries a small grey link at the top right saying how many slides the run costs and where it lets out. Clicking it jumps straight there, which is the mouse equivalent of d for one run rather than all of them.

5.2 Part 1 – Credibility and tools (90′)

Rel. Segment What I do What participants do Watch out for
0:00 Welcome + the two kinds of aim + what you do not need (6′) Premise in two sentences (“Nature asked three times whether social science holds up – I was inside one of the papers; today you sit where I sat”). Walk the two-column aims slide. “Open the lab tab / your workspace NOW.” Open lab tab (engine warms) or Positron workspace; type one word in chat for why they came. Late joiners: greet in chat, never restart. Two-device tip again.
0:06 Talk: what science is for, and why it is called a crisis (10′) What a claim is for; the three findings that made people nervous (Simmons, Gelman & Loken, Breznau); why it is structural rather than fraud. End on “you cannot solve it by hiring better people – so suppose you did”. Listen. This segment is untimed in the room.
0:16 Talk: the first many-analysts study (4′) Silberzahn’s red-card study, which is the answer to the question that the previous slide just asked: the question, the dataset, the design – twenty-nine teams reviewing each other’s approaches before anyone saw a result. Then all twenty-nine estimates, grouped by the response distribution that each team assumed. End on the left column: “hold on to that grouping, we come back to it twice”. Watch. Do not narrate the 29 rows. The two things to land are the blind peer-review step and the distribution grouping; everything else is scenery. This is the only place where the study gets a proper telling – Part 2 shows it again in one line, as a reminder.
0:20 Talk: the vocabulary, and what open research fixes (8′) Why ‘replication’ covers three different operations; the data × analysis matrix; credibility is not truth; what open research proposes and what it does not fix. The matrix is the one slide to copy down. If it runs long, the open-research practices table compresses to one sentence per row. The red-card study is what makes the vocabulary problem concrete – refer back to it rather than re-arguing it.
0:28 Talk: SCORE and its three studies (20′) The programme; the three corners; Miske → Aczel → Tyner, each with its result; the three Rs are uncorrelated; the credibility-in-the-wild quiz pair. Chat waterfalls on the quiz. If behind, cut the Miske moderators slide to one line.
0:48 Talk: Multi100 from the inside (14′) What an analyst was sent; the paper in one slide (define OA here); the two tasks and why that order; how a hundred studies become one number; then the five-analyst forest one row at a time, ending on “look at the labels”. Watch the reveal. Never rush the forest reveal. The software column sets up the tools segment – do not talk over it.
1:02 Stretch (2′) Cameras off, stand up. Stretch. Actually stop talking.
1:04 Talk: the tools (18′) What open source buys you; the toolchain wall; languages and editors (optional depth); plain text and the render pipeline; packages; version control; where data and code are archived; licences; what we use today; what the tools cannot do. Note what they might switch to. The timing here is tight. The per-tool slides are the compressible part. The two that must land are plain text is the format that survives and what the tools cannot do – the second leads into Part 2.
1:22 Task 1 launch + break (8′) Set the setup task: “get to the point where a line of R runs and the data are in front of you – take the break for it if you need to”. Post break-return time; countdown slide. Stay on the call through the break for STUCKs. Start Task 1; break. This is the only part of the day where a broken machine can be fixed without costing anyone content. Use it.

5.3 Part 2 – The claim and its estimand (90′)

Rel. Segment What I do What participants do Watch out for
0:00 Talk: the paper, in full (20′) The four framing scales; what OA actually fitted; the robustness question already inside the paper (Model 1 vs Model 2); what we work on instead and what the aggregate cannot answer; the two failed reproductions; the sign-flipped replication; the three-Rs scoreboard; then the five free-phase questions table. Listen; ask. The Model 1 / Model 2 slide is the one that people remember – give it its beat.
0:20 Talk: estimands (25′) “Two studies to set beside our five” – Silberzahn’s 29 estimates return as a one-line strip, so this is a reminder and not a retelling → “they were not answering the same question” → A&B’s four estimands → fix the estimand and the spread collapses → the vague-research-questions slide → Lundberg’s three steps. Listen; ask. The contrast figure is the pay-off of this segment: the top row is the real 29 estimates, and its spread is carried by two outliers. Say so – it is the difference between a scary picture and an argument.
0:45 Stretch (2′) Cameras off, stand up. Stretch. Actually stop talking.
0:47 Talk: DAG crash course (25′) Confounder/mediator/collider; open/closed paths; total effect or direct effect; selection and feedback; then Revenons à nos moutons – back to our own claim, and the chat exercise. Estimand waterfall there: read 3–4 aloud, point at the spread. Then why draw the graph when identification is out of reach; publishing the estimand and the graph; end on the half-built graph and its dagify() code panel. Type their one-sentence estimand; hold; send on “go”. Revenons sits here on purpose: it is the return to our own data after the digression through somebody else’s football match, and it lands harder for having waited. Silent room: name two people gently, or take reactions; two answers from twenty is a proceed. The estimand waterfall is never compressed. The two slides on drawing the graph without identification are what stop this segment feeling like a bait-and-switch on an observational panel.
1:12 Hands-on 2: Task 2 (18′) Optionally walk part of the R tutorial first (10 optional-depth slides) – pitch it at whoever is least confident. Then: meet the panel, write the estimand, uncomment the arrows they believe and run adjustmentSets(). Look at the data; write the estimand; finish the DAG; two-sentence FE note. Trigger at 1:22: if the room is behind, switch to demo-only – run it on my screen, they note the output. The picture cell in the lab installs ggdag (~23 MB, one slow moment on poor wifi); the adjustment set itself needs none of it.
1:30 Lunch launch Achievement line; lunch slide; post return time. Lunch. Lunch is a hard stop. Anything unfinished in Task 2 can be finished over lunch.

Think; argue in chat. The target answer: “we can’t say until we know what chance produces on these dependent data.” Someone will get there – let them say it.
0:55 Critics → one correct analysis → preregistration (10′) The three critical positions; Lakens’ one-correct-analysis claim; granting the argument; what preregistration settles and what it does not; then the OSF segment (three addresses, the scavenger beat if time allows, the registered protocol filed by Multi100 itself, the AsPredicted mapping). Scavenge if run; read; ask. If the clock is tight, the scavenger beat is the first thing to drop – the mapping table is what Task 5 needs.
1:05 Hands-on 5: THE PREREGISTRATION MOMENT + run + report (20′) Called from the front, synchronous: complete fields 1–5 → render the block, or save it into the script on the browser lab page → type “declared”. 7 Chat protocols and ready-to-paste texts

Waterfall: “Type your answer now but DON’T send – hold it. … Go.” Holding the answers back keeps each one independent of the others, and releasing them together is what produces the wall-of-identical-values beats.

Help: a STUCK in chat is the first flag. The standing help doc – a shared editable doc whose link goes into the chat at open – is the second surface, and the place where errors get pasted. Check it at the minutes that the run sheet names, not continuously. Rescues run in the main room by buddy-chunk: their spec, my webR or Positron, and everyone watching learns from it.

Silent room: wait 20 seconds, then take reactions (thumbs) as the minimal response, and name one or two people gently (“Sam, what did you get?”). Two substantive answers from twenty participants is a proceed, not a failure.

Catch-up card (paste at Part 3 re-entry):

Just back? You missed nothing you can’t recover: (1) if Task 2 (the estimand and the DAG) is unfinished, run its DAG chunk as-is and come back to the wording later; (2) Task 3 is the reproduction – the target is t = −3.804 and there is a full solution on the page; (3) Task 4 is exploration, so just try two specifications and submit one. Flag STUCK and I’ll come to you in the run window.

Facilitator-drop standing order (seeded in chat at open):

If I drop off the call: keep going. The day plan (link) has the timings – finish the current task or take the break early. I will rejoin as soon as I can.

8 The cutting order

Every optional-depth run is a compression lever before anything else is. Take time from a tutorial before taking it from a task.

  • Part 1 – trigger 0:30: cut the Miske moderators slide to one line and compress the open-research practices table to one sentence per row. Trigger 1:05: drop the two per-tool optional-depth slides (languages, editors) and the licences slide; keep plain-text-survives and what-the-tools-cannot-do.
  • Part 2 – trigger 0:50 (running long after estimands): the DAG crash course drops the selection-and-feedback slide to one sentence and reaches the half-built graph sooner. Trigger 1:12: skip the R tutorial entirely and go straight into Task 2. Trigger 1:22: Task 2 to demo-only – run it on my screen, they note the output and finish over lunch. The estimand waterfall is never compressed.
  • Part 3 – trigger 0:57: the methodological tutorial drops to its three core slides (outcome, claim alignment, estimators). Trigger 1:10: Task 4 shortens to two try_spec() calls and a submission – but everybody still submits, because Part 4 opens on those dots.
  • Part 4 – trigger 0:33: skip the multiverse tutorial, keep ‘what the curve leaves out’. Trigger 0:55: drop the scavenger beat and lean the inference module on the reading. The class-chart debrief, the fork-importance reveal and the preregistration moment are never compressed.

Never cut: the five-analyst reveal and its software column · the estimand waterfall · the half-built DAG and its adjustment set · the reproduction waterfall · the Task 4 submission · the preregistration moment · the fork-importance reveal.

9 Contingencies

Each row names the first move, not the only one, and every ladder ends at a point from which the session can carry on. Nothing in the table stops the day.

Situation What to do
A participant’s webR fails mid-task The cheatsheet ladder: hard reload + rerun chunks above → second browser → buddy-chunk on my screen → grid lookup + hand submission (any spec looks up in data/spec_grid_full.csv, the full 2,520-row universe). Everyone lands a dot.
A participant’s installation breaks Switch them to the browser lab, which holds the whole exercise; their workspace survives for later.
The live chart fails during the day (Form, Worker, CORS) Rung 1: results.qmd falls back to the committed data/class_results.csv automatically. Rung 2: pull the Sheet CSV, commit, re-render locally, share my screen. Rung 3: the static 840-spec curve + five-analyst slides still carry the debrief. Submissions are safe in the Sheet regardless – nothing upstream blocks on the chart.
Breakout rooms unavailable Every pair moment has a scripted chat variant. Zero structural cost.
OSF slow/unreachable during the scavenger Show h7432 on my screen; the beat survives. Data never depends on OSF live – it ships with the workspace and the browser lab.
My connection drops The standing order in chat: participants continue the current task or break early; I rejoin. The pipeline keeps collecting – it is entirely independent of the call.
A participant cannot speak/hear Everything essential is chat-and-screen; deliver any spoken instruction in chat on request.
Recording objections Recording is optional by design – stop it; nothing depends on it.
Prereg mirror form fails The rendered block is the record; carry on.

10 Expected numbers

  • Warm-up matrix question set: 1 = reproducibility (a failed attempt is still reproducibility) · 2 = the grey area between reproducibility and robustness – argue both ways · 3 = replicability · 4 = generalisability.
  • Task 3 (the workspace and the browser lab, unchanged): b = −0.00347, SE = 0.00091, t = −3.804, df = 233, N = 270 (EUframes_cy.csv) – the value on record for C6HJR, reproduced directly from euframes.
  • The data-preparation material is off the running order and out of the deck. If you want to raise it on the day, the facts, the withdrawn slides and their speaker notes are in _planning/block2-error-story-withdrawn-2026-07-30.md, local to your machine – paste any block straight back into the deck.
  • Family axis (a first-class menu axis; not part of the Multi100 Task-2 menu, which fixed unemployment and, implicitly, the gaussian scale): beta submissions report z, not t. In the browser lab every spec, beta included, is read from the pre-computed grid, so report_result() takes no fit; the workspace fits beta live. Grid-lookup fallback: any spec looks up in its shard under data/grid/ – the file is named by the six Level 1 choices, the row by the other eight; the cheatsheet has the recipe. The beta family is pre-computed at the canonical measurement settings only. Beta twin of C6HJR’s published Multi100 specification: b(logit) = −0.01399, z = −4.075, r = −0.258 (C6HJR’s Gaussian r = −0.242: same direction, still significant, slightly stronger).
  • Claim-alignment (the room’s convention, set on the spec menu): negative framings (mcomm, mlib, mneg) reverse-coded 1 − y before fitting; sign of b, t and r flips, magnitudes and p untouched (exactly so for beta too). Aligned r runs −0.70 to +0.24 (gaussian; beta reaches +0.29) – so a class dot above ≈ +0.25 is almost certainly an un-recoded negative framing, not a genuine contrary result; name that in the debrief. One caveat from the growth-predictor axis: legitimately aligned growth-predictor dots can reach ≈ +0.37 – check the declared spec before calling it.
  • Analyst placement (share of grid more negative, claim-aligned): KEVF1 15.8% · KQXUE 21.5% · C6HJR 49.6% · 018OL 63.1% · PRL47 67.9%. The two person-level analysts (KEVF1, PRL47) sit at the extremes; the constrained baseline lands almost exactly at the median of the aligned grid.
  • Task-1 layer (slide after analyst placement): free phase = five different questions – 018OL GDP p.c. level, KEVF1 growth, PRL47 a GDP battery; only KQXUE and C6HJR used unemployment – with one shared recorded verdict (‘evidence for the claim’). No standardised Task-1 effect sizes exist in the public Multi100 data (verdict + free text only). My own Task 1 (mposframe ~ growth + unemp, two-way FE, World Bank series) reproduces from the committed as-submitted panel: unemployment t = −4.72, growth ns – standardisation weakened my result.
  • Predictor axis (a first-class menu axis; menu + lab lookup in the data/grid/ shards; at the canonical measurement settings the growth half is 840 cells across both families; not part of the Multi100 Task-2 menu, though Task 1 shows three of five analysts freely chose GDP measures): GDP growth as claim carrier, raw form only – affine forms are t-inert (centred≡raw, log-z≡log), which is why the deduplicated canon holds 840 distinct analyses. Growth predictor (840 rows in the full grid): 70.5% claim-direction / 46.9% significant, aligned max +0.37 – direction without significance, matching KQXUE’s Task-1 growth null. Growth mirror of my Task-1 model on the EU-frames panel: b = +0.00167, t = 1.59, ns. Growth polarity: claim-aligned reporting flips the sign (lab lookup does it).
  • Fork importance: outcome 49.7% · residual/interactions 49.2% · sample 0.7 · estimator 0.4 · predictor form/copredictor/weights ≈ 0.
  • Sign flips (mcosmo universe, 140 specs): 41 wrong-sign; 33 in pooled_cl, 8 in fe_twoway; 24 of the 41 in bailout-excluding samples (halved from the pre-dedupe counts – every distinct analysis appeared exactly twice before, so shares are invariant).
  • Constrained universe: 140 specs · 70.7% negative · 57.1% negative-and-significant. Discussion-beat target: “no verdict without a null for dependent specifications – that is what a curve-level permutation test would supply (the design in the inference reading).”
  • Baseline percentile (for participants without their own submission): −3.804 ⇒ ≈ 49.6% on the claim-aligned scale.

11 Debrief questions

Choose by what the class chart actually shows, not by the order below.

  1. Sign flip: “Did anyone’s specification flip the sign? If not – after seeing the flips chart, where would you have had to stand in the menu to flip it?”
  2. Significance loss: “Did any spec lose significance? Reason to doubt the claim, or to scrutinise that spec?”
  3. Axis that moved most: “Before I showed the decomposition – which axis did you bet on, and why did most of us get it wrong?”
  4. Justification quality: “Whose DAG settled their choice? Whose DAG could have justified three different specs equally well?”
  5. Preregistration felt: “Did completing the block before running change how the result felt when it landed? Would you have chosen differently after seeing it?”
  6. Robustness tolerance: “Multi100 used ±0.05 and ±0.20 d. Is the claim robust by the tight standard here? The loose one? Who gets to choose the tolerance for a policy claim?”
  7. Against the five analysts: “The class overlaps the five analysts’ range. Is that reassuring or circular? What would a result outside their range have meant?”
  8. Percentile literacy: “Your percentile places you among 840 defensible alternatives. What does a 10th-percentile analyst owe their reader that a 50th-percentile analyst does not?”
  9. The three Rs on one paper: “This paper failed reproduction, survived robustness in sign but not magnitude, and sign-flipped in replication. Which R should worry us most – and does it depend on what the finding is used for?”
  10. The inference gap: “If a curve-level permutation test came back at p = .30, what would survive of today? What if it came back at p = .004?”
